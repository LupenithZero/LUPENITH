# Ethics and Conduct: Harm, Responsibility, and Fair Process

**Status:** Draft. This is an internal ethical framework, not a substitute for civil or criminal law.

The project may use the word **“wrongdoing”** or, in a future philosophical context, **“sin.”** Neither word should be used to police harmless thoughts, private beliefs, or lawful personal choices. The system should focus on conduct that violates rights, causes unjustified harm, or abuses entrusted power.

## 1. Core distinction

- **Beliefs and private thoughts:** not misconduct by themselves.
- **Mistakes and disagreement:** usually call for discussion, learning, or correction—not punishment.
- **Carelessness:** assessed according to context, predictability of harm, duty of care, and available alternatives.
- **Intentional or reckless harm:** requires a serious response proportionate to the risk and evidence.
- **Abuse of authority:** aggravating factor because it can harm people and undermine fair process.

## 2. Categories of serious misconduct

Examples include, subject to evidence and context:

### Harm, coercion, and exploitation
- Violence, credible threats, stalking, or deliberate intimidation.
- Sexual abuse, harassment, or conduct without legally and ethically meaningful consent.
- Coercion, extortion, trafficking, or exploiting a person's dependency.
- Deliberate actions that expose others to serious, foreseeable danger without justification.

### Deception and financial abuse
- Fraud, theft, embezzlement, undisclosed conflicts of interest, or deliberate misrepresentation for material benefit.
- Misusing shared funds or claiming to provide services, loans, jobs, or protections that do not exist.
- Pressuring members to donate, work without agreed terms, recruit others, or surrender assets as proof of loyalty.

### Privacy and security violations
- Doxxing, unauthorized access, exposure of private data, or sharing sensitive information without a lawful and justified basis.
- Retaliation against someone who responsibly reports a security flaw or safeguarding concern.
- Knowingly misrepresenting a product as secure or private while concealing material risks.

### Abuse of organizational power
- Manipulating elections or vote records.
- Retaliating against critics, whistleblowers, or people who leave.
- Using rank to demand personal obedience, sexual access, money, private labor, or isolation from outside relationships.
- Interfering with investigations, destroying relevant records, or using AI scores to bypass due process.

### Serious professional or safeguarding failures
- Deliberately misrepresenting qualifications in a sensitive role.
- Violating child-safeguarding obligations or concealing suspected abuse.
- Publishing unsafe health claims while falsely presenting them as established medical guidance.

This list is not exhaustive. New categories should be proposed publicly and assessed for clarity, rights impact, and misuse risk.

## 3. What must not count as a sin or offense by itself

- Criticizing the founder, leadership, charter, or majority.
- Rejecting the movement's religious or philosophical framing.
- Having a private religious belief or being non-religious.
- Leaving, pausing participation, refusing a task, or declining to donate.
- Making a good-faith mistake or changing one's mind after new evidence.
- Having an illness, disability, trauma history, financial hardship, or needing professional help.
- Engaging in a lawful, consensual private choice simply because another member dislikes it.

## 4. Proportionate responses

Depending on severity and context, responses may include:

1. Education, feedback, or correction for low-risk mistakes.
2. Mediation or restorative processes where participation is voluntary and safe.
3. A documented warning or correction request.
4. Temporary limitation of a specific role or access while risk is reviewed.
5. Removal from a role or community after fair process for serious or repeated misconduct.
6. Referral to qualified professionals or lawful authorities when appropriate and necessary.

Do not impose financial penalties, public humiliation, forced confessions, or restrictions on personal freedom without a clear legal basis and due process. The organization has no authority to replace courts or professional regulators.

## 5. Fair process

For consequential allegations:

- Provide a clear description of the alleged conduct and relevant rule.
- Protect the privacy and safety of reporting and responding parties.
- Preserve relevant evidence and distinguish facts from speculation.
- Give the respondent a reasonable opportunity to reply.
- Use an impartial reviewer with disclosed conflicts of interest.
- Explain the decision and why the response is proportionate.
- Provide an appeal route to a different reviewer or body.
- Protect good-faith reports from retaliation while also discouraging deliberate, malicious fabrication through fair, evidence-based procedures.

Do not treat an allegation as proof. Do not require a victim to confront an alleged abuser directly as a condition of being heard.

## 6. Health, pleasure, and self-care

The project supports informed decisions that protect long-term health and agency. It does not shame people for enjoying pleasure, taking prescribed medication, receiving medical care, or struggling with habits. Advice should distinguish personal values from scientific evidence and should not promise that discipline alone can overcome every illness or structural barrier.

Content creators should not diagnose viewers, advise them to stop prescribed treatment, or present a general video as individualized care. High-risk content must be reviewed by suitably qualified professionals.

## 7. Children and vulnerable people

Any future child-related program must have an independently reviewed safeguarding policy, required checks and training under local law, safe reporting routes, appropriate supervision, privacy protections, and professional oversight. Children must never be recruited into ideological work or made dependent on belief in order to receive care. Their safety, rights, voice, and future autonomy come first.

## 8. Review standard

Before adopting a new offense or sanction, ask:

- What specific harm or right violation does this address?
- Could this rule be abused to silence dissent or enforce conformity?
- Is the rule clear enough that people can predict what is prohibited?
- Is the response proportionate and appealable?
- Could education, prevention, or repair work better than punishment?
