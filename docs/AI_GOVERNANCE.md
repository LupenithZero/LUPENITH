# AI Governance Principles

**Status:** Draft. No organizational AI system currently exists, and no AI has authority over members.

AI may help a global community manage information, translate material, compare proposals, summarize public records, identify contradictions, and support research. It can also reproduce errors, bias, manipulation, and false confidence. The community must therefore design AI as an assistive tool, not a sovereign authority.

## Permitted uses to explore

- Summarizing public proposals with links to original material.
- Finding duplicate issues and helping organize a public knowledge base.
- Translation assistance with human review for consequential text.
- Checking draft policies against a published checklist.
- Analyzing aggregate, privacy-preserving feedback.
- Helping identify research sources, while requiring people to verify the sources and claims.
- Flagging possible conflicts of interest or inconsistent records for human review.

## Uses that should be prohibited or tightly restricted

- Automatic final punishment, expulsion, leadership removal, or denial of essential support.
- Secret loyalty, personality, belief, or “moral worth” scores.
- Predicting who is a dissenter, “bad member,” or likely enemy.
- Covert monitoring of private conversations or member devices.
- Replacing clinical assessment, legal judgment, or child-safeguarding professionals.
- Generating fake votes, fake identities, testimonials, or fabricated evidence.
- Making high-impact decisions from sensitive attributes or opaque scores without a lawful, necessary, and independently reviewed basis.

## Decision rule

For any consequential decision affecting a person's rights, role, access, safety, or reputation:

1. An AI output may be considered only as one source of information.
2. The person affected must receive a meaningful explanation of the issue and a chance to respond, subject to legitimate safety constraints.
3. A qualified and accountable human decision-maker must review the evidence.
4. The decision must be recorded with reasons and an appeal route.
5. A different reviewer should handle the appeal.
6. Known limitations, failure rates, data sources, and conflicts of interest should be documented where relevant.

No AI should be the sole judge of its own performance, its own authority, or the founder's conduct.

## Collective intelligence without privacy collapse

“Built from collective intelligence” must not mean uploading every member's private conversations, health data, identity documents, or personal history into a model. Participation in data collection must be informed and voluntary, and privacy-preserving alternatives should be preferred. Use aggregate or de-identified information only where suitable, remembering that de-identification can fail.

Members must be told what data is collected, why, who can access it, whether it is used for model training, how long it is kept, and how they can withdraw where feasible.

## Audit and change control

Any future AI used in governance should have:

- A clear owner and stated purpose.
- Versioned prompts, models, evaluation criteria, and change logs where practical.
- Test cases for bias, adversarial manipulation, privacy leakage, and failure modes.
- Independent review before high-impact use.
- A human override and shutdown procedure.
- A means to report errors and appeal decisions.
- No hidden vendor access or undisclosed use of member data.

## Public communication

Never claim that an AI is objective, unbiased, infallible, conscious, or more legitimate than democratic decision-making without strong evidence—and never use such claims to demand obedience. AI-generated recommendations should be labeled where that matters to users, and consequential claims must be independently checked.
